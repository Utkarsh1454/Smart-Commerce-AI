Abstract The rapid growth of electronic commerce has resulted in the generation of massive volumes of customer interaction and advertisement impression data. Extracting meaningful patterns from these high-throughput streams requires scalable computational architectures capable of supporting both personalized product recommendations and accurate advertisement click-through rate (CTR) estimation. Traditional single-node and single-threaded processing paradigms encounter computational and memory bottlenecks when handling high-dimensional behavioural datasets and training iterative machine learning models. This paper presents Smart Commerce AI, an integrated parallel recommendation and advertisement prediction framework developed using Apache Spark 3.5.1 and PySpark MLlib. The proposed framework unifies two complementary machine learning engines: an implicit-feedback collaborative filtering model based on Alternating Least Squares (ALS) for personalized product recommendations, and supervised classification models (ElasticNet Logistic Regression and Random Forest) for advertisement click-through rate prediction. The recommendation engine incorporates confidence-weighted customer interactions, encompassing product views, favourites, cart additions, and purchases. The advertisement prediction component evaluates behavioural, demographic, and contextual features while enforcing strict temporal filtering to prevent data leakage.

All empirical experiments were conducted in a local multi-threaded parallel execution mode (local[*]) on a single multi-core host. The reported evaluation includes ranking measures, classification metrics, execution throughput, and partition-level scalability benchmarks. The experimental results document an ALS NDCG@10 of 0.0197, catalogue coverage of 52.50% (recommending 1,008 unique items across 1,920 candidates, representing a 101× gain over the 0.52% coverage of a Popularity Baseline), a Logistic Regression ROC-AUC of 0.5830, and a Random Forest log-loss of 0.5963 (with positive F1 of 0.2433). Controlled partition benchmarks demonstrate a maximum speedup of 1.50× using four partitions before Spark task-scheduling overhead incurs diminishing returns. The study clarifies the trade-offs of local parallel execution, delineates dataset limitations, and presents concrete architectural blueprints for production e-commerce pipelines.

1 Introduction

Electronic commerce platforms have transformed modern consumer discovery, evaluation, and purchasing workflows. Contemporary commercial environments generate continuous streams of user behavioural logs, encompassing browsing sessions, item search queries, wish-list additions, shopping cart actions, checkouts, and advertisement displays (Zhou et al. 2018). Processing these interaction streams provides opportunities to understand granular customer preferences and deliver timely, personalized product recommendations and sponsored advertisements.

Personalized recommender systems represent a cornerstone of modern digital storefronts. Their primary objective is to surface catalog items that maximize consumer relevance and catalog exploration without requiring explicit numerical ratings. Collaborative filtering approaches are widely adopted because they uncover latent behavioral relationships between users and items directly from interaction patterns (Sarwar et al. 2001; Hu et al. 2008). Concurrently, sponsored computational advertising introduces a critical complementary challenge: estimating the Click-Through Rate ($P(\text{Click} = 1 \mid \mathbf{x})$) of candidate advertisements to dynamically weight merchant bids in automated second-price auctions (He et al. 2014; McMahan et al. 2013).

The escalating volume of interaction records renders these dual workloads computationally demanding. Traditional single-node processing methods frequently struggle with memory exhaustion, high garbage collection latency, and unscalable execution times when processing iterative matrix factorizations and multi-field feature crosses. Apache Spark provides an expressive cluster-computing framework designed to parallelize large-scale data workflows through in-memory Resilient Distributed Datasets (RDDs), Catalyst query optimization, Spark SQL, and the MLlib distributed machine learning library (Armbrust et al. 2015; Zaharia et al. 2016).

This research introduces Smart Commerce AI, an integrated parallel recommendation and advertisement prediction framework developed using Apache Spark 3.5.1 and PySpark MLlib. The framework unifies: (1) an implicit-feedback Alternating Least Squares (ALS) recommendation engine for organic product discovery, and (2) an imbalance-mitigated advertisement click prediction engine comparing ElasticNet Logistic Regression and Random Forest classifiers. Importantly, this investigation focuses on establishing baseline parallel execution efficiency, in-memory DAG transformations, and multi-core resource utilization within a local multi-threaded host environment (local[*]), avoiding unfounded extrapolations to distributed physical clusters.

2 Background and Related Work

2.1 Collaborative Filtering and Implicit Feedback

Collaborative filtering (CF) algorithms infer user preferences by analyzing historical interaction overlap across customer cohorts (Su and Khoshgoftaar 2009). While early recommendation literature predominantly addressed explicit feedback (e.g., 1-to-5 star ratings in the Netflix Prize dataset), enterprise e-commerce platforms operate almost exclusively on implicit feedback gathered passively from customer actions (page views, clicks, cart additions, purchases) (Koren et al. 2009).

Implicit feedback presents unique structural challenges: (1) no negative feedback is explicitly recorded, as unobserved user-item pairs reflect either active disinterest or unawareness; (2) interactions are inherently noisy; and (3) numerical values indicate interaction frequency and confidence rather than direct preference magnitude. Hu, Koren, and Volinsky (2008) introduced a seminal confidence-weighted matrix factorization model that separates binary preference ($p_{ui} \in \{0, 1\}$) from monotonically increasing confidence ($c_{ui} = 1 + \alpha r_{ui}$). Alternating Least Squares (ALS) solves this non-convex factorization by iteratively fixing user latent vectors while optimizing item vectors via closed-form ridge regression, and vice versa. Spark MLlib implements block-partitioned ALS, enabling parallel coordinate updates across memory partitions (Meng et al. 2016).

2.2 Advertisement Click-Through Rate Prediction

Sponsored advertising engines must forecast the exact conditional probability of an ad interaction: $\text{CTR}(\mathbf{x}) = P(y = 1 \mid \mathbf{x})$, where $y \in \{0, 1\}$ denotes whether an impression resulted in a click, and $\mathbf{x}$ encapsulates user profile attributes, ad metadata, and historical behavioral summaries. Logistic Regression with ElasticNet regularization remains a commercial baseline due to its extreme inference throughput and probabilistic interpretability (McMahan et al. 2013; Chapelle et al. 2014). To capture non-linear relationships, ensemble tree models such as Random Forest (He et al. 2014) and Gradient Boosted Decision Trees (Chen and Guestrin 2016) partition the feature space into hierarchical decision regions. While modern deep CTR architectures (e.g., Wide & Deep, DeepFM, DIN) capture high-order latent interactions, tree ensembles in Spark MLlib provide a highly competitive balance between predictive calibration and native distributed CPU training without requiring external GPU infrastructure.

2.3 Distributed Computing with Apache Spark

Apache Spark has emerged as the premier engine for large-scale data analytics and distributed machine learning (Zaharia et al. 2016). Spark structures execution via a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) of stage boundaries demarcated by data shuffle operations. The Catalyst query optimizer automatically applies relational optimizations, including predicate pushdown, projection pruning, and Broadcast Hash Joins (BHJ) (Armbrust et al. 2015). Smart Commerce AI leverages these capabilities to combine data preparation, model training, and analytical evaluation.

3.3 Spark-Based Data Preprocessing

Data preprocessing is performed using PySpark. The pipeline loads behavioural and advertisement datasets, validates input records, joins advertisement information with user profiles, aggregates behavioural interaction counts, encodes categorical attributes, constructs machine learning feature vectors, and persists the processed data in Parquet format. Spark SQL is used for structured transformations and joins, and Broadcast Hash Joins are enforced for smaller dimension tables to eliminate shuffle exchanges.

A critical operational safeguard implemented in Smart Commerce AI is the strict elimination of temporal data leakage. For every advertisement impression, historical behavioural features are calculated exclusively from actions occurring prior to the impression timestamp:

$\mathcal{B}_{\text{valid}}(u, c, t_{\text{imp}}) = \{ b \in \texttt{behavior\_log} \mid b.\texttt{user\_id} = u \ \land \ b.\texttt{cate\_id} = c \ \land \ b.\texttt{time\_stamp} < t_{\text{imp}} \}$ (1)

Actions occurring at or after the impression timestamp ($t \ge t_{\text{imp}}$) are strictly excluded, eliminating target leakage.

3.4 Product Recommendation Using Implicit ALS

The recommendation engine uses implicit-feedback collaborative filtering. Customer interactions are converted into numerical weights to represent different levels of engagement, as listed in Table 2.

Table 2: Interaction weights applied by the recommendation engine.
Interaction Action Event Code (btag) Assigned Weight $w(e)$
Product view (pv) pv 1.0
Favourite (fav) fav 3.0
Cart addition (cart) cart 4.0
Purchase (buy) buy 5.0

For a user $u$ and item $i$, the aggregated interaction strength is:

$r_{ui} = \sum_{e \in \mathcal{E}_{ui}} w(e)$ (2)

The preference value is defined as:

$p_{ui} = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } r_{ui} > 0 \\ 0 & \text{if } r_{ui} = 0 \end{cases}$ (3)

and the confidence value is:

$c_{ui} = 1 + \alpha r_{ui}$ (4)

where $\alpha = 20.0$. The ALS objective function minimizes confidence-weighted squared error with Tikhonov regularization:

$\mathcal{L} = \sum_{u,i} c_{ui} (p_{ui} - \mathbf{x}_u^T \mathbf{y}_i)^2 + \lambda \left( \sum_u \|\mathbf{x}_u\|_2^2 + \sum_i \|\mathbf{y}_i\|_2^2 \right)$ (5)

where $\mathbf{x}_u$ and $\mathbf{y}_i$ represent user and item latent factor vectors, respectively, and $\lambda$ is the regularization parameter. The implementation uses the configuration given in Table 3.

Table 3: Hyperparameter configuration for Spark MLlib Implicit ALS.
Parameter Identifier Description Configured Value
Latent factor rank ($d$) Dimensionality of latent factor representations 12
Iterations Alternating optimization sweeps 10
Regularization parameter ($\lambda$) Tikhonov $\ell_2$ shrinkage coefficient 0.08
Confidence scaling parameter ($\alpha$) Weight assigned to observed behavioral signals 20.0

3.5 Advertisement Click Prediction

The advertisement prediction engine estimates the probability of a click based on user, advertisement, and behavioural features. The processing pipeline applies median imputation for missing numerical values, string indexing for categorical attributes, and feature vector construction using VectorAssembler, followed by the training of Logistic Regression and Random Forest classifiers.

The Logistic Regression model uses ElasticNet regularization with a maximum of 30 iterations and a regularization parameter of 0.05. The Random Forest classifier uses 60 decision trees, a maximum tree depth of 8, and random seed 42. The output is a predicted click probability:

$\text{CTR}(u, a) = P(\text{click} = 1 \mid \mathbf{x}_{u,a})$ (6)

3.6 Advertisement Ranking and Recommendation Integration

The recommendation engine generates organic product candidates, while the advertisement engine estimates the click probability of sponsored candidates. An expected-value-oriented advertisement score is used to rank sponsored candidates:

$\text{eCPM}_i = 1000 \times \text{bid}_i \times \text{pCTR}_i$ (7)

Here, $\text{bid}_i$ represents the advertiser's cost-per-click bid, while $\text{pCTR}_i$ represents the predicted click probability expressed as a decimal. The resulting eCPM estimates the expected advertising value per thousand impressions under the assumed cost-per-click model.

4.2 Recommendation Evaluation Metrics

The product recommendation engine is evaluated using five measures: Precision@$K$ measures the proportion of recommended items that are relevant, while Recall@$K$ measures the proportion of relevant items successfully retrieved within the top-$K$ recommendations. Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain (NDCG) evaluates ranking quality while assigning greater importance to relevant items appearing near the beginning of the recommendation list. Mean Average Precision (MAP) measures ranking performance through the average precision of recommendation lists. Finally, catalogue coverage measures the proportion of available items that appear in at least one recommendation list.

4.3 Dataset Splitting and Experimental Protocol

The verified experimental protocol extracted directly from the codebase (src/train.py) is documented as follows:

  • Split type: Deterministic random split using PySpark's DataFrame.randomSplit().
  • Random seed used for the split: Globally set to seed = 42 across all data partitioning and model initializations.
  • Validation set: In the initial baseline run, an 80/20 train/test split without a validation set was used. In the refined pipeline, a dedicated 15% validation partition ($N_{\text{val}} = 2,158$ records) was extracted alongside a 70% training split ($N_{\text{train}} = 10,617$) and a 15% test split ($N_{\text{test}} = 2,225$) specifically to tune classification decision thresholds independently of test data.
  • Classification threshold: $\tau = 0.50$ for the unweighted baseline; tuned on validation data to $\tau^* = 0.40$ for Logistic Regression and $\tau^* = 0.30$ for Random Forest.
  • Both models evaluated on the identical split: Yes. Both Logistic Regression and Random Forest were evaluated on the identical test split.

4.4 Advertisement Classification Metrics

The advertisement prediction models are evaluated using ROC-AUC, PR-AUC, classification accuracy, positive-class precision, recall, F1-score, and log-loss. Log-loss is particularly relevant when the predicted click probability is used in advertisement ranking because it evaluates probability calibration.

4.5 Partition-Level Performance Evaluation

Partition-level performance is evaluated using execution time, speedup ($S_p = \bar{T}_1 / \bar{T}_p$), and parallel efficiency ($E_p = S_p / p$).

6 Discussion

6.1 Recommendation Quality and Catalogue Coverage

The ALS model provides an effective mechanism for discovering relationships between customers and products based on historical interaction patterns. The reported catalogue coverage of 52.50% confirms that recommendation lists include a broad range of candidate items, overcoming the severe popularity bias of the baseline (0.52%). However, the relatively low absolute NDCG (0.0197) and precision values reflect high matrix sparsity (> 98%), indicating that coverage reflects inventory exposure diversity rather than high conversion relevance.

6.2 Advertisement Prediction Trade-Offs

The CTR experiments demonstrate that Logistic Regression and Random Forest provide different advantages. Logistic Regression achieved slightly higher ROC-AUC (0.5830) and recall (0.9593), whereas Random Forest achieved significantly lower log-loss (0.5963 vs. 0.6856) and higher positive F1 (0.2433). This difference is vital because advertisement ranking depends directly on the reliability of predicted click probabilities for expected value auction scoring.

6.3 Parallel Processing Overhead

The partition benchmark demonstrates that a greater number of partitions does not monotonically improve execution performance. For relatively small in-memory datasets, Spark scheduling, task serialization, and thread synchronization overhead can exceed actual computation time. A larger dataset and multi-node cluster would be necessary to evaluate scale-out network performance.

6.4 Limitations

The current implementation has several limitations: (1) experiments were performed on a single multi-core host using local[*] rather than a physical multi-node cluster; (2) the demonstration dataset (15,000 ad samples) is smaller than the industrial 26-million Taobao log; (3) user behavior was aggregated at category-brand level; and (4) CTR discrimination remains modest (ROC-AUC ≈ 0.58), reflecting the need for deep sequential feature architectures.

7 Conclusion and Future Work

This paper presented Smart Commerce AI, an integrated parallel e-commerce recommendation and advertisement click prediction framework developed using Apache Spark 3.5.1 and PySpark MLlib. The system combines implicit-feedback ALS for personalized product recommendation with Logistic Regression and Random Forest classifiers for advertisement click prediction.

The reported results demonstrate an ALS NDCG@10 of 0.0197 and catalogue coverage of 52.50% (101× gain over the Popularity Baseline). For advertisement prediction, Logistic Regression achieved a ROC-AUC of 0.5830, while Random Forest achieved a calibrated log-loss of 0.5963. The partition benchmark demonstrated parallel execution trade-offs under Amdahl's Law. Crucially, the experiments confirm that CTR classification on compact tabular features exhibits modest discriminative power (ROC-AUC ≈ 0.58), highlighting the necessity of deep sequential architectures for industrial deployment.

Future work will focus on: (1) evaluating the framework on a true multi-node physical Spark cluster using the full RecZoo TaobaoAd_x1 dataset; (2) integrating Apache Kafka and Spark Structured Streaming for real-time model updates; and (3) deploying dedicated low-latency serving endpoints with attention-based deep interest networks.

8 Detailed System Implementation and Execution Architecture

8.1 Physical Execution Plan and Broadcast Hash Joins

To evaluate how Spark Catalyst query optimization impacts data preparation, physical execution plans were inspected via the .explain() API. In e-commerce pipelines, relational joins between wide transaction logs (raw_sample) and dimension catalogs (ad_feature, user_profile) represent a primary computational bottleneck. A standard join triggers SortMergeJoin, which incurs complete partition sorting and network shuffle exchanges:

$\text{Cost}_{\text{SMJ}} = \mathcal{O}(N_{\text{sample}} \log N_{\text{sample}} + N_{\text{dim}} \log N_{\text{dim}}) + \text{NetworkTransfer}(N_{\text{sample}} + N_{\text{dim}})$ (8)

By wrapping dimension catalogs with F.broadcast(), Catalyst transforms the physical plan into a BroadcastHashJoin:

== Physical Plan == *(2) BroadcastHashJoin [adgroup_id#12], [adgroup_id#45], Inner, BuildRight :- *(2) Project [user_id#10, adgroup_id#12, time_stamp#14, clk#18] : +- *(2) Filter (isnotnull(time_stamp#14) AND isnotnull(adgroup_id#12)) : +- FileScan parquet [user_id#10,adgroup_id#12,time_stamp#14,clk#18] +- BroadcastExchange HashedRelationBroadcastMode(List(cast(input[0, int, false]))) +- *(1) Filter isnotnull(adgroup_id#45) +- FileScan parquet [adgroup_id#45,cate_id#46,campaign_id#47,price#50]

The BuildRight directive constructs an in-memory hash table of the dimension data within the driver and broadcasts it to all parallel worker threads. Worker tasks perform $\mathcal{O}(1)$ local memory lookups without generating a single shuffle file, reducing join latency from 12.4s to 5.8s.

8.2 End-to-End Dual-Engine Serving Integration Flow

The dual-engine integration operates through a decoupled serving pipeline. When customer $u$ accesses the digital storefront:

  1. Organic Retrieval: The ALS latent factor model performs a matrix dot product between user vector $\mathbf{x}_u$ and candidate item matrix $\mathbf{Y}$, retrieving the top-10 items with highest predicted preference $\hat{p}_{ui} = \mathbf{x}_u^T \mathbf{y}_i$.
  2. Sponsored Candidate Scoring: Active candidate advertisements matching user demographics are transformed through the fitted Random Forest pipeline, yielding calibrated click probabilities $\text{pCTR}_i$.
  3. eCPM Auction Ranking: Expected advertising value is calculated ($\text{eCPM}_i = 1000 \times \text{bid}_i \times \text{pCTR}_i$).
  4. Safeguard Filtering: Window functions (Window.partitionBy("user_id", "campaign_id")) enforce a frequency cap of at most 2 ads per campaign and eliminate ads with invalid pricing ($\text{price} \le 0$).
  5. Storefront Presentation: Sponsored candidates populate designated promotional placements, while organic recommendations populate the personal discovery carousel.

The confusion matrix exposes the fundamental trade-off of learning under acute class imbalance. Lowering the decision threshold ($\tau^* = 0.30$ for Random Forest) enables the model to capture 92.59% of true clicks ($\text{TP}=250$) at the expense of a high False Positive Rate ($\text{FPR}=78.5\%$). 9.2 Probabilistic Calibration and Log-Loss Divergence

To evaluate probability calibration, test impressions were partitioned into predicted probability deciles. The empirical cross-entropy loss is formulated as:

$\text{Log-Loss} = -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \left[ y_i \ln(\hat{p}_i) + (1 - y_i) \ln(1 - \hat{p}_i) \right]$ (9)

While Logistic Regression achieved a marginally higher ROC-AUC (0.5830 vs. 0.5822), its Log-Loss (0.6856) was degraded by uncalibrated probability predictions near 0 and 1. Conversely, Random Forest averages predictions across 60 independent decision trees, smoothing probability estimates and yielding a Log-Loss of 0.5963 (a 13.0% improvement over linear regression). In second-price auctions, where bid scaling is proportional to $\hat{p}_i$, calibrated probabilities prevent advertiser over-bidding.

10 Reproducibility, Threats to Validity, and Deployment Considerations

10.1 Reproducibility Protocol and Determinism

To facilitate external replication, all experimental code, preprocessed datasets, and evaluation scripts are publicly archived (Pandey 2026). Full determinism is maintained by fixing random seeds across all randomized transformations:

  • Dataset partitioning: randomSplit([0.7, 0.15, 0.15], seed=42).
  • Random Forest feature bagging: RandomForestClassifier(seed=42).
  • ALS factor initialization: deterministic latent vector initialization in PySpark MLlib.

10.2 Threats to Validity

  • Internal Validity: Potential threats include feature leakage and evaluation bias. We eliminated leakage through formal point-in-time filtering ($t_{\text{behavior}} < t_{\text{impression}}$) and unit testing. Threshold optimization was performed strictly on validation data to prevent optimistic test evaluation.
  • External Validity: The demonstration dataset utilizes 15,000 impressions and category-brand groupings. While structurally identical to industrial AliMama schemas, behavioral dynamics on full-scale platforms (e.g., millions of users and SKU-level catalogs) may exhibit higher variance.
  • Construct Validity: Recommender performance was measured via offline ranking metrics (NDCG@10, coverage). While indicative of algorithmic quality, offline metrics cannot capture online user exploration behavior or real-time conversion elasticity.

10.3 Production Deployment Considerations and Safeguards

Deploying dual recommendation and advertising architectures in commercial environments requires strict operational safeguards:

  1. Ad Frequency Capping: Repeatedly displaying identical campaigns causes user fatigue. We enforce a strict constraint limiting exposure to at most 2 impressions per campaign per user session.
  2. Inventory and Price Integrity: The pipeline validates item metadata, enforcing $\text{price} > 0.0$ and checking SKU availability before auction participation.
  3. Data Privacy Compliance: User demographic attributes (gender, age_level) are processed strictly as anonymized categorical indices, complying with privacy frameworks (e.g., GDPR) by prohibiting Personally Identifiable Information (PII) ingestion.

11 Extended Future Directions

Future extensions of Smart Commerce AI encompass three technological vectors: (1) streaming architecture deployment using Spark Structured Streaming and Delta Lake for sub-second latent factor updates; (2) deep sequential feature extractors (e.g., Deep Interest Networks) operating on user behavioral sequences; and (3) privacy-preserving federated matrix factorization across edge client nodes.
